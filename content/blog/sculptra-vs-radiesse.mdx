Sculptra and Radiesse are both collagen stimulators, and that is about where the similarity ends. Sculptra works only gradually, over a short course of sessions. Radiesse gives some support on the day and then works gradually too. Neither can be dissolved afterwards, which is the point most comparisons leave out.

Patients often come to me having read that one is "better". That is the wrong question. They are made of different materials, they behave differently in the skin, and they suit different people and different areas. This guide explains how each works, where they differ, and what a doctor weighs before choosing either, or neither.

## What is Sculptra?

[Sculptra](/technology/sculptra) is made by Galderma. Its active material is poly-L-lactic acid (PLLA), a synthetic, bioresorbable material long used in absorbable stitches. It comes as a dry powder that the clinic mixes with sterile water before use.

Once injected, the fluid is absorbed fairly quickly, so whatever you see on the day mostly goes down within days. The PLLA particles are then broken down slowly, and while that happens they act as a stimulus for your own collagen. That is why Sculptra is planned as a course of sessions a few weeks apart, with change appearing over weeks to months rather than at the appointment.

## What is Radiesse?

[Radiesse](/technology/radiesse) is made by Merz Aesthetics. Its active material is calcium hydroxylapatite (CaHA), tiny mineral-like microspheres suspended in a gel.

The gel gives support straight away, then is absorbed. The microspheres stay in the tissue for a period and act as a scaffold around which your body forms collagen. Radiesse can also be thinned with saline, usually called hyperdilute Radiesse, and spread more widely to work on skin quality rather than shape. Whether standard or diluted preparation suits you is a clinical decision.

## Which one suits which concern?

Generally, Sculptra is considered where the concern is overall firmness and thinning across a region, such as the cheeks or temples, in someone happy to build change slowly. It suits people who prefer a gradual result that friends do not notice overnight.

Radiesse is more often considered where some structural support is wanted as well, for example along the jawline or in deeper folds, and it is the one of the two with labelling for the back of the hands and the décolletage.

In practice there is a lot of overlap, and some plans use neither. If the main concern is a single line or a specific volume loss that you want corrected on the day, a [hyaluronic acid filler](/treatments/dermal-fillers) may be the more suitable tool, and it has the advantage of being dissolvable. If skin quality is the main concern, a skin booster may be considered first. Our [bio-stimulator treatment](/treatments/bio-stimulator) page explains where this category fits, and there is a separate comparison of [Ellansé and Sculptra](/blog/ellanse-vs-sculptra), the other bio-stimulator patients often ask about.

## Why "cannot be dissolved" matters

With a hyaluronic acid filler, an enzyme can break the product down if the result is not right or a complication needs treating. Neither Sculptra nor Radiesse has an equivalent. Once placed, the material is cleared by the body on its own timetable.

That does not make either unsafe. It changes how carefully the plan should be made. I would rather under-treat and review than over-treat something that cannot be taken back, and I tell patients to expect a conservative first session for that reason.

## Risks and who should avoid them

Both carry the usual injectable effects: swelling, redness, tenderness and bruising at the injection sites, which commonly settle within days. Both can, less often, cause small lumps or nodules under the skin. With Sculptra these have been reported months after treatment; with Radiesse the manufacturer notes they are more likely on the hands. Rare but serious vascular complications are recognised for facial injectables in general, which is one reason these are doctor-performed procedures.
