Skin boosters and microneedling are often mentioned in the same breath, because both are used for skin quality rather than shape. They work in different ways. A skin booster is an injection that places a hydrating or regenerative material into the skin. Microneedling, as offered at Kaiteki, is radiofrequency (RF) microneedling: fine needles deliver controlled energy at set depths to prompt the skin's own renewal.

This guide compares the two side by side: how each works, what each is usually considered for, what recovery involves, whether they can be part of one plan, and who should not have either.

## What is a skin booster?

A [skin booster](/treatments/skin-booster) is a series of small injections that deliver a product into the skin. "Skin booster" is an umbrella term, and the products under it are not interchangeable. At Kaiteki they include hyaluronic acid boosters such as [Profhilo](/technology/profhilo) and Hydrodeluxe, polynucleotide boosters such as [Rejuran](/technology/rejuran) and Plinest, and a collagen-stimulating booster, [Juvelook](/technology/juvelook).

A 2024 overview in *Archives of Plastic Surgery* describes the category the same way: different biological materials grouped under one name, used for dullness, dehydration and loss of elasticity rather than for adding volume. That last point matters. A skin booster does not change the shape of the face.

## What is RF microneedling?

[RF microneedling](/treatments/microneedling) uses a handpiece of very fine needles. As they enter the skin, they deliver radiofrequency energy at a depth the doctor sets, creating tiny controlled zones of heat that prompt collagen renewal through the deeper skin. Kaiteki uses three devices with different reach and modes: [Potenza](/technology/potenza), Morpheus8 and [Sylfirm X](/technology/sylfirm-x).

Plain microneedling, without energy, is also offered at some clinics, sometimes under a brand name such as a dermapen. It is a different procedure from RF microneedling, so it is worth asking which one is meant when the two are compared.

## Skin booster vs RF microneedling at a glance

| | Skin booster | RF microneedling |
| --- | --- | --- |
| What it is | An injectable product placed into the skin | Fine needles delivering radiofrequency energy |
| What it leaves behind | A material that the body clears over time | Nothing; the effect comes from the skin's response |
| Commonly considered for | Hydration, dull or dehydrated-looking skin, fine lines linked to dryness, sensitised skin | Depressed acne scarring, texture, enlarged pores, etched lines |
| Numbing | Topical anaesthetic, usually | Numbing cream for around 30 to 45 minutes |
| Recovery listed on our pages | 1 to 2 days | 2 to 3 days |
| Usual plan | A short course, then maintenance | A course, spaced a few weeks apart |
| Skin tone | Not light-based, so tone is not the limiting factor | RF is not absorbed by melanin, so it is used across a broad range of tones |

## What recovery looks like

After a skin booster, small raised bumps at each injection point are expected, with possible redness, mild swelling and pinpoint bruising. The bumps settle as the product disperses: within hours to about a day for hyaluronic acid, and over roughly one to two days for polynucleotides. After a collagen-stimulating booster, they can take a few days.

After RF microneedling, the skin is usually red, warm and flushed for the first one to two days, often with a faint grid pattern from the needle tips. Some dryness or light flaking can follow, and deeper settings can leave the skin tender for slightly longer.

Both need gentle skincare and sun protection afterwards. A review in *Dermatologic Surgery* found RF and RF microneedling carried a low risk of scarring or darkening in skin of colour, but temporary pigment change is still possible, particularly with poor sun protection.

## Which one suits which concern?

Generally, a skin booster is considered when the main concern is hydration and overall skin quality, or skin that needs support after irritation or other treatment. RF microneedling is more often considered for [acne scars](/concerns/acne-scars), uneven texture and [enlarged pores](/concerns/enlarged-pores), where the aim is renewal through the deeper skin.

There is overlap. Juvelook, for example, is a booster used for pores and texture too, and for some people neither is the right first step: active acne, for instance, is usually settled medically first.

## Can they be combined?

Sometimes both are part of one plan, because they work through different mechanisms. They are usually done on separate visits, with the order and spacing set by your doctor after assessing your skin. Combining is not automatic: one may be enough, and adding a second procedure adds its own recovery and risks.
