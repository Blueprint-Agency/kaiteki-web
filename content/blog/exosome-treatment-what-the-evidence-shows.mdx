Exosomes arrived on aesthetic menus faster than the evidence did. In a few years they went from a cell-biology term almost nobody outside a lab used, to a line item in clinics across Kuala Lumpur, usually bolted onto microneedling or a laser and usually described in language that runs ahead of what has been shown in people.

This guide separates the three things that get blurred together: what exosomes are, what clinics actually do with them, and what human studies and regulators have said so far. Parts of it are genuinely promising. Parts of it are unsettled. One part is not a grey area at all.

## Key takeaways

- **What they are:** nanoscale vesicles released by cells, carrying proteins, lipids and RNA that act as messages between cells.
- **What clinics do:** most apply an exosome preparation to the surface straight after microneedling or a laser, while channels in the skin are briefly open. That is a different procedure, and a different regulatory question, from injecting them.
- **What the evidence shows:** small human studies report improvements in skin quality and hair density. The trials are few, short, and hard to compare with each other.
- **Where regulators stand:** the US FDA has approved no exosome product for any use. The UK medicines regulator treats injected exosomes as a medicine and says they cannot lawfully be given in aesthetic procedures there.
- **What that means for you:** the sensible questions are what product is used, what it is derived from, and what the doctor expects it to add over the procedure it is paired with.

## What exosomes actually are

Exosomes are extracellular vesicles: tiny membrane-bound parcels, roughly 30 to 150 nanometres across, that almost every cell in the body releases. They are not cells and they do not divide. They are cargo carriers, packed with proteins, lipids and RNA, and their job is signalling. One cell packages a message, releases it, and a neighbouring cell takes it up.

That signalling role is the entire basis of the interest in aesthetics. If you can deliver the signals a young, healthy cell sends without delivering the cell itself, the reasoning goes, you might nudge repair processes in skin or a hair follicle. It is a coherent idea, and it is well supported in laboratory and animal work.

The gap is between that mechanism and a measured result in a person. Mechanistic plausibility is where a treatment starts, not where it finishes. Several treatments with excellent mechanistic stories have gone on to show very little in controlled trials.

One distinction matters more than the marketing usually admits: exosomes are not stem cells, and a product being "stem cell derived" describes where the vesicles were harvested from, not what is in the bottle. Sources vary widely between products, including human mesenchymal stem cells, animal cells and plant material such as rose stem cells. Different source, different contents, different regulatory status.

## How exosome treatment is actually done

In practice, exosome preparations are almost always a companion to something else rather than a treatment on their own.

The common protocol is to run a procedure that briefly opens micro-channels in the skin, then apply the exosome preparation to the surface while those channels are open. That paired procedure is usually [microneedling](/treatments/microneedling) or a resurfacing laser. The channels are what get a large molecule past the skin barrier, which it would not otherwise cross in any meaningful quantity. On the scalp the same logic applies, with microneedling used to get the preparation past the same barrier.

This matters for reading the evidence. When a study reports improvement after microneedling with exosomes, some of that improvement belongs to the microneedling, which has its own established effect on collagen. Very few studies have compared the pair against the procedure alone, which is exactly the comparison that would tell you what the exosomes contributed. Our [exosome therapy](/treatments/exosome-therapy) page sets out how the treatment is planned around the procedure it accompanies.

The other route, injecting exosome preparations into the skin, is the one that carries the regulatory problem set out below.

## What the human evidence shows

The honest summary is that the direction of the early findings is encouraging and the quality of the evidence is not yet strong.

**For skin.** Reviews published through 2025 and 2026 in journals including *Aesthetic Surgery Journal* and *Journal of Cosmetic Dermatology* have pooled the human studies of exosome-based treatments in aesthetic medicine. What they find is a small literature: on the order of a dozen or so clinical studies, mostly single-arm, with participant numbers frequently in the tens rather than the hundreds and follow-up commonly measured in weeks or a few months. Reported outcomes lean towards skin texture, tone and hydration, alongside acne scarring and pigmentation. A scoping review in dermatology put it plainly, describing the clinical landscape and the safety picture as not yet defined.

**For hair.** The alopecia literature is at a similar stage. A 2024 review of clinical studies of exosomes for hair loss concluded that very limited human data on safety and efficacy exist, and a later systematic review across alopecia types and exosome sources reached the same holding position: efficacy and safety remain to be determined. Individual studies do report increases in hair density and thickness, which is why interest persists, but the studies are small and the products differ from one to the next. If hair is your concern, our [hair loss](/concerns/hair-loss) page covers the options with a longer evidence record.

**The comparability problem.** This is the one that undermines every attempt to draw a firm conclusion. There is no standardised exosome product. Two clinics offering "exosome therapy" may be using preparations that differ in source cell, isolation method, particle count, purity and storage. A result obtained with one preparation does not transfer to another, and reviews consistently name this heterogeneity as the reason findings cannot be pooled with confidence. It also means the phrase on the menu tells you almost nothing on its own.

## Where regulators stand

This is the part that gets left out of most articles on the subject, and it is the part a patient most needs.

**United States.** The FDA issued a Public Safety Notification on Exosome Products on 6 December 2019, stating that there are no FDA-approved exosome products, after multiple reports of serious adverse events in patients in Nebraska treated with unapproved products marketed as containing exosomes. Those events included severe infections requiring hospitalisation. That position has not changed: no exosome product is FDA-approved for aesthetic use.

**United Kingdom.** The MHRA regards exosomes, when injected, as a medicinal product. No exosome injectable holds a UK marketing authorisation, so injecting them in an aesthetic procedure is not lawful there. Topical use after a procedure sits differently, and the distinction between the two is the whole point.

**Malaysia.** Cell and gene therapy products fall under the NPRA's registration framework, which classifies products by risk. Regenerative treatments are provided in private clinics under a registered doctor's responsibility, which places a heavier burden on the individual clinic's sourcing and on the consultation itself than a settled approval pathway would.

None of this makes exosome preparations useless. It does mean that anyone presenting them as an established, proven treatment is describing something other than the current state of the evidence.

## Who this is not suitable for

Exosome treatment is not appropriate for everyone, and suitability is a clinical judgement rather than a preference.

- It is generally not offered during pregnancy or breastfeeding.
- Active infection, inflammation or a broken skin barrier at the treatment site needs to settle first, since the accompanying procedure breaches the barrier by design.
- Certain skin and scalp conditions, medications and medical histories change the calculation, which is why the full history matters at consultation.
- If your primary concern is significant laxity or volume loss, a regenerative add-on is not the tool for the job. Our [ageing](/concerns/aging) and [fine lines and wrinkles](/concerns/fine-lines-wrinkles) pages set out what actually addresses those.

Anyone whose interest in the source of the product is religious or ethical rather than clinical should ask directly what the preparation is derived from, because the answer differs between products and is not something you can infer from the treatment name.

## Questions worth asking before you book

If you take one thing from this guide, take this list into the consultation.

| Ask this | Why it matters |
| --- | --- |
| What product are you using, by name? | "Exosomes" is a category, not a product. The specifics are the only way to look anything up. |
| What is it derived from? | Human, animal and plant-derived preparations are different propositions, clinically and otherwise. |
| Is it applied topically or injected? | These are separate procedures with separate risk and regulatory profiles. |
| What is it paired with, and what does the pairing do on its own? | Tells you how much of the expected change is attributable to the exosomes. |
| What does the evidence support for my concern? | A straight answer here, including where evidence is thin, is a good sign. |

A doctor who answers those five without reaching for superlatives is giving you a better basis for a decision than any article can. You can review our [doctors](/doctors) and their credentials before booking.

## So is it worth considering?

As an addition to a procedure that already has its own evidence, discussed with realistic expectations, it is a reasonable thing to talk through. As a standalone answer to ageing or hair loss, presented as proven, it is ahead of the science.
