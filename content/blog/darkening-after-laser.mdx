Some people notice a laser-treated area turning darker rather than lighter a week or two after treatment. In the medium to deeper skin tones common in Malaysia this is usually post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation (PIH): the skin answering the treatment's inflammation with extra pigment. It is common, it is usually treatable, and the first thing to do is stop it getting worse.

This guide is for when darkening has already appeared. For how to look after your skin straight after a laser, see [what to avoid after laser treatment on your face](/blog/after-facial-laser-aftercare).

## Why skin darkens after laser

Every laser causes some inflammation, and in skin with more melanin, pigment cells tend to respond to inflammation by producing more pigment. A review in the *Journal of Clinical and Aesthetic Dermatology* describes PIH as more frequent, more intense and longer-lasting in skin of colour, including Asian skin. Settings that are too strong for the skin type, sun exposure during healing, picking at crusts and irritating skincare all add to the inflammation.

Darkening is a known risk of laser treatment on Asian skin, which is why doctors here tend to choose gentler settings and longer gaps between sessions. It does not always mean something went wrong, but it does mean the plan needs to change.

## Is it PIH, melasma or something else?

Not every darker patch after a laser is the same thing, and they are handled differently.

| | Post-laser darkening (PIH) | Melasma flare |
|---|---|---|
| Where | Matches the treated area, sometimes the exact pattern of the handpiece | Symmetrical patches across both cheeks, forehead or upper lip |
| When | Appears days to a few weeks after treatment | Can follow treatment, sun, heat or hormonal change |
| Course | Usually lightens over months with care | Chronic, tends to come back with triggers |

If you already had [melasma](/concerns/melasma), a laser can sometimes make it flare, and that needs a more cautious plan than ordinary PIH. A doctor examines the skin, sometimes under magnification, before deciding what to do.

## What to do now

### Protect it from light every day

Use a broad-spectrum sunscreen every morning and reapply when outside. A tinted sunscreen adds some protection against visible light, which also drives pigment in deeper skin tones. Hats and shade matter more than usual for the next few months.

### Calm the skin, then treat it

Keep the routine gentle: a mild cleanser and a plain moisturiser, and stop anything that stings. Once the skin is calm, a doctor may prescribe topical treatment that slows pigment production. Prescription lightening agents work, but they can irritate, and irritation feeds PIH, so they are used under supervision.

### Give it time, and keep follow-ups

Medscape's clinical overview notes that clearing PIH is often a slow process measured in months. Some marks lighten on their own with sun protection alone; others need treatment. Nobody can promise in advance how long yours will take.

## What doctors may add

Where topical care and sun protection are not enough, a doctor may consider low-energy treatment, such as [Pico Laser](/treatments/pico-laser) at conservative settings, spaced well apart. A 2024 systematic review in the *Journal of Cutaneous Medicine and Surgery* found that every treatment for PIH in skin of colour, laser included, works only partly and inconsistently, and that laser itself sometimes made PIH worse. That is why it is a careful, later step rather than the first response.

Other kinds of [pigmentation](/concerns/pigmentation), such as sun spots, behave differently, which is another reason the diagnosis comes before the device.
